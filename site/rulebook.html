Part two

Who owns what

The FTC holds the brand responsible for creator content — even things a creator says that we never told them to say. So the structural work sits with us, and creators carry only three simple duties.

Freestyle owns
  • Substantiation — current testing on file for every factual claim before it appears anywhere; retest on product or competitor changes
  • The approved claims list — one canonical, versioned source of exact language
  • Onboarding & contracts — guidelines acknowledged in writing (the Growi e-sign exhibit) before the first post; FTC clause in every agreement
  • Monitoring & correction — rotating spot-checks, the 48-hour fix loop, everything logged
  • Reviews Rule hygiene — nothing ever conditioned on sentiment; no undisclosed insider reviews; no suppression
  • Employee policy & our own ad copy — per Parts six and seven, plus legal review of campaign claims
Creators own (3 things)
  • Disclose on every post — clearly, at the top, spoken in video; free product and commissions count
  • Be honest — genuine use, genuine opinions
  • Stay inside the lines — own experience in their own words; no danger words, stats, or competitor comparisons
A creator who does these three has near-zero personal risk — the FTC pursues brands, not individual influencers, outside egregious repeat cases. Substantiation, the Green Guides, the FDA line, and the FCC are never their problem.

The workflow the partnerships team runs

  1. Onboard — guidelines sent → e-signed acknowledgment via the Growi contract → records kept.
  2. Equip — current approved claims list shared; creators notified when it changes (briefs stay stat-free).
  3. Post — creator publishes with disclosure.
  4. Spot-check — rotating sample of tagged content against the checklist, every creator at least once per quarter.
  5. Fix — non-compliant post → correction request → confirmed within 48 hours → logged.
  6. Escalate — repeat issues pause the partnership (documented); borderline claims go to legal before posting.

Part three

Claim rules, by selling point

The master rule: every factual claim needs current substantiation on file before it ships, anywhere — packaging, site, ads, creator captions. Approved claims are used verbatim; a paraphrased substantiated claim is an unsubstantiated one.

Absorbency ("55% more absorbent than the #1 brand")

Allowed: the exact tested claim, with the basis of comparison disclosed near it ("vs. [brand], size X, [test]") — and only while the testing is current.
Never: "most absorbent on the market," rounded numbers, "up to" stretching, comparing our best size against a competitor's weakest, or letting creators restate the stat in their own words.

Why: NAD killed Coterie's "up to 4x vs. leading brands" because the testing didn't match the words. Define "#1 brand" precisely; retest when we or competitors change product. Expect P&G or Kimberly-Clark to challenge us the same way.

Bamboo & plant-based (BambooTek™)

Allowed: "core made from 100% bamboo pulp" — qualified to the component it's true of.
Never: implying the whole diaper is bamboo or plant-based, "all-natural," or unqualified "plant-based" without saying which parts.

Why: closures, SAP, and backsheet aren't bamboo. Unqualified material claims draw Green Guides scrutiny.

Free-of & non-toxic

Allowed: specific free-of statements with testing on file ("free of fragrances and phthalates"), re-verified on every material or supplier change.
Never: "chemical-free," safety scare comparisons ("other diapers are toxic"), or keeping "free of allergens"/"hypoallergenic" language live after a composition change without re-testing.

Why: the Huggies class action is exactly this — a quiet materials change under a standing "hypoallergenic" label. Lock change-control between R&D and marketing.

Skin & diaper rash

Allowed: "helps keep skin dry," "designed for sensitive skin," dryness-mechanism framing.
Never: "prevents / treats / cures diaper rash" or any medical outcome; "dermatologist-tested / pediatrician-approved" without the study on file.

Why: prevent/treat/cure language makes the diaper an unapproved drug under the FDA's skin-protectant monograph. One word separates a product claim from a drug claim.

Environmental

Allowed: specific, qualified facts — "the core is bamboo, a fast-growing plant"; quantified claims ("X% less plastic than…") only with a documented baseline.
Never: biodegradable, compostable, eco-friendly, sustainable, zero-waste, "good for the planet," flushable, "saves trees" without numbers.

Why: gDiapers settled FTC charges over "biodegradable"/"flushable" diapers. We currently hold no substantiation for general environmental-benefit claims.

Safety & superlatives

Allowed: factual, tested statements about our own product.
Never: "safest diaper," "no harmful ingredients whatsoever," "the only diaper you can trust," or anything implying competitors hurt babies.

Why: absolute safety is unsubstantiable, and implied-safety superiority is exactly what BBB National Programs flagged in infant-care advertising guidance.

Part four

The danger words

Banned in all copy — packaging, site, paid media, social captions, and creator content — unless legal signs off on a specific substantiated exception:

Never, anywhere
  • prevents / treats / cures / heals (any condition)
  • hypoallergenic · free of allergens (without current testing + legal sign-off)
  • chemical-free · 100% natural · all-natural
  • biodegradable · compostable · eco-friendly · sustainable
  • flushable
  • safest · guaranteed · never leaks
  • doctor / dermatologist / pediatrician recommended (no study on file)
  • unapproved competitor comparisons or made-up statistics

Part five

Creator program rules

We are legally liable for what creators post — including things we never told them to say. Free product and affiliate commissions both count as "material connections," so gifting-only and commission-only creators must disclose like paid ones. Our duties:

Guidelines before the first postEvery creator receives the Playbook (or 3 Rules brief) and acknowledges it in writing. Keep the records.
Contracts carry complianceFTC-compliance clause plus the approved-claims annex in every agreement.
One approved claims listCanonical, versioned, exact language with qualifiers. Creators quote verbatim or don't use it.
Monitor for realMonthly spot-checks of tagged content, minimum. Unenforced guidelines count against us in an investigation.
Fix fast, log everythingNon-compliant post → correction request → confirmed within 48h → documented. Repeat offenders paused or terminated, documented.
Disclosure at the first touchpointNo "door-opener" content — a post that looks like an impartial review can't push the disclosure to the landing page it links to.

Part six

Reviews (the 2024 Reviews Rule)

Civil penalties run ~$50k+ per violation, and this rule reaches marketing ops directly:

  • Never pay for, or condition anything on, a positive review — including "leave a review to stay in the program."
  • Never create, buy, or seed fake or AI-generated reviews.
  • Never suppress or filter out negative reviews on our own site while presenting the set as complete.
  • Product-seeding programs that invite retail reviews must bake the disclosure into the ask.
  • Insider reviews (employees, family) without a disclosed relationship are violations — see below.

Part seven

Employees & family

  • Any employee — or their immediate family — posting about Freestyle discloses the employment relationship, every time ("I work at Freestyle" / "my partner works at Freestyle").
  • Employees and immediate family never post retail-site reviews of Freestyle products. At all.
  • Employee posts follow the same claim rules as everything else in this rulebook.
If we ever buy broadcast: radio/TV spots and host-read endorsements trigger FCC sponsorship-identification rules on top of everything here — loop in legal before booking.

Part eight

The worry list, ranked

  1. Comparative absorbency claims — the most likely NAD challenge from P&G/Kimberly-Clark. Substantiation file airtight and current, always.
  2. Rash-language drift — "keeps skin dry" becoming "cured the rash" in creator captions. FDA drug-claim line.
  3. "Free of allergens"/hypoallergenic exposure — class-action bait on any composition change.
  4. Undisclosed gifting & affiliate links — the highest-volume failure; creators assume free product doesn't count. It does.
  5. Eco-claim creep — "sustainable"/"biodegradable" sneaking into captions or our own social copy.
  6. Reviews Rule slip-ups — incentivized or insider reviews without disclosure.
