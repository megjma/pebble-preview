# Freestyle Advertising Claims — Internal Regulatory Brief

**Audience:** Freestyle marketing, partnerships, and leadership (internal only — the companion doc `freestyle-creator-guidelines.md` is the creator-facing piece).
**Prepared:** August 2026. Not legal advice; validate final claim language with advertising counsel.

---

## 1. Who actually regulates diaper advertising

| Regulator / body | What they cover | Relevance to Freestyle |
|---|---|---|
| **FTC** | Deceptive/unsubstantiated advertising (FTC Act §5), endorsements & influencers (Endorsement Guides, 16 CFR 255, revised June 2023), fake/incentivized reviews (Consumer Reviews & Testimonials Rule, 16 CFR 465, effective Oct 21, 2024), environmental claims (Green Guides, 16 CFR 260) | **Primary regulator.** Covers all product claims, all influencer content, all reviews |
| **FDA** | Drug claims. "Prevents/treats diaper rash" is an OTC drug claim under the Skin Protectant monograph (M016 / 21 CFR 347) — legal only for monograph drug products (zinc oxide, petrolatum, etc.), not diapers | Governs the line between "keeps skin dry" (OK) and "prevents diaper rash" (drug claim) |
| **FCC** | Broadcast radio/TV only: sponsorship identification (47 U.S.C. §317, 47 CFR 73.1212) — paid placements on broadcast must be announced by the station | **Mostly not our problem.** Only applies if we buy broadcast spots or host-read radio endorsements. Social, streaming, podcasts, and digital = FTC, not FCC |
| **NAD/CARU (BBB National Programs)** | Industry self-regulation; competitors file challenges; non-compliance gets referred to the FTC | **Very active in diapers** — P&G challenged Coterie in 2025 and won. Expect incumbents to police our comparative claims |
| **State AGs & class-action bar** | State UDAP statutes, consumer class actions | Huggies is currently defending a class action over "hypoallergenic" claims after a material change caused reported reactions |

**Bottom line:** For influencer/creator content, the FTC is essentially the whole game; the FCC only enters if we do broadcast. The FDA line matters for how *everyone* (us and creators) talks about diaper rash.

## 2. Freestyle's selling points, claim by claim

### "55% more absorbent than the #1 brand" / "hyper absorbent" (BambooTek™)
- Comparative superiority claims require **head-to-head testing that matches the claim**: same sizes, current competitor products, reliable methodology, results that hold across the brands referenced.
- **Precedent:** NAD v. Coterie (June 2025, challenge by P&G). Coterie's "Up to 4x absorbency vs. leading brands" was killed because testing covered only Huggies (not Pampers), "up to" didn't save it, and it compared its best diaper against a competitor's weakest. NAD also required endorsement disclosures at the first touchpoint.
- **Action:** Keep the substantiation file current (retest when we or competitors change product), define "#1 brand" precisely, and disclose the basis of comparison near the claim. Never let creators paraphrase the stat.

### "100% bamboo pulp core, not viscose" / "plant-based"
- Fine if literally true **of the core** — but the claim must not imply the *whole diaper* is bamboo/plant-based (closures, SAP, backsheet, etc.). Qualify: "core made from 100% bamboo pulp."
- "Plant-based" without qualification invites Green Guides scrutiny about which components and what percentage.

### "Non-toxic," "free of phthalates, fragrances, allergens"
- "Free-of" claims are permitted if (a) the substance is genuinely absent (testing on file), (b) the product doesn't contain a substitute posing similar risk, and (c) the claim doesn't imply competitors' products are unsafe.
- "Free of allergens" is aggressive — effectively a hypoallergenic promise. The Huggies class action shows the exposure: a composition change plus "hypoallergenic" labeling = alleged fraud. If we keep this claim, lock change-control between R&D and marketing so the claim is re-verified on any material/supplier change.
- "Non-toxic" needs substantiation covering foreseeable exposure routes (skin contact, mouthing). Never extend it to "chemical-free" or competitor disparagement.

### "Protects against moisture and diaper rash better than every other plant-based diaper"
- Two problems stacked: a **superiority claim** (needs testing across the plant-based category, per Coterie) and a **rash-adjacent claim**. "Helps protect against diaper rash" via dryness is defensible for a diaper if framed as a moisture/dryness mechanism; "prevents/treats diaper rash" crosses into FDA drug-claim territory (Skin Protectant monograph M016 reserves that language for zinc oxide/petrolatum-type products).
- **Action:** Prefer "helps keep skin dry, which helps protect against diaper rash" with category-wide substantiation for any "better than" framing — or drop the comparative.

### "Safe for even the most sensitive skin"
- Absolute safety claims ("safe for even the most…") are hard to substantiate and are exactly what BBB National Programs flagged in its 2026 "infant care claims" guidance (implied-safety and superiority messaging in wipes/diaper advertising). Prefer "designed for sensitive skin" + any actual clinical testing ("dermatologist-tested" only if we have the study).

### Eco/sustainability ("saving trees," "removing plastic from the environment")
- Green Guides require substantiation for general environmental-benefit claims; unqualified "biodegradable" needs complete breakdown within ~1 year in customary disposal — impossible for landfill-bound diapers. **gDiapers (Down to Earth Designs) settled FTC charges in 2014** over "100% biodegradable"/"flushable" diaper claims.
- "Saves trees" and "removes plastic" need a quantified, documented basis (vs. what baseline?). Prefer specific, qualified claims: "core made from bamboo, a fast-growing plant" / "X% less plastic than our previous design."

## 3. Influencers: our liability, their liability

### What Freestyle is on the hook for (this is what to worry about)
Under the revised Endorsement Guides, the **advertiser** is liable for:
1. **Undisclosed material connections** in creator content — even if we never told the creator to skip the disclosure. "Material connection" includes free product, not just cash.
2. **Claims creators make on our behalf** — a creator saying "cured my baby's eczema" or "3x more absorbent than Pampers" is *our* unsubstantiated claim.
3. **Failing to monitor.** The FTC expects a real program: written guidance (✅ the companion doc), training/acknowledgment, ongoing monitoring of posts, and documented follow-up/termination for non-compliance. A guidelines PDF we never enforce is worse than useless in an investigation.
4. **Reviews Rule violations** (civil penalties, currently ~$50k+ per violation, inflation-adjusted): buying/selling fake reviews, incentives conditioned on positive sentiment, undisclosed employee/insider reviews, review suppression. This covers employees and their **immediate relatives** posting reviews without disclosure.
5. **Door-opener deception:** disclosure must appear at the first consumer touchpoint (the social post), not just on the landing page/blog it links to (per the Coterie decision).

**Recommended program mechanics:** contract clause requiring FTC compliance + approved-claims annex; onboarding acknowledgment of the guidelines; monthly spot-checks of tagged content; a takedown/correction SLA; records of everything.

### What non-employee creators genuinely don't need to worry about
Share this framing with partners — it lowers the temperature:
- **Substantiation is our job, not theirs.** If they quote an approved claim exactly, the testing burden sits with Freestyle. They don't need lab data for their own honest, first-hand experience ("we've had fewer blowouts").
- **The FCC is irrelevant to them.** Sponsorship-ID rules bind broadcast stations, not social creators.
- **Green Guides / FDA monograph mechanics** — they never need to learn them; they just need to avoid the banned words list (biodegradable, compostable, prevents/treats/cures, chemical-free, hypoallergenic, safest).
- **Personal enforcement risk is low if they disclose and stay honest.** The FTC has historically pursued brands, not individual influencers, except in egregious repeat cases. An endorser's core duties are just: (1) disclose the relationship, (2) actually use the product, (3) only state genuine opinions/experiences, (4) don't relay claims they have reason to doubt.
- **They are NOT exempt from disclosure** just because they're not employees — gifted product alone triggers it. (Employees have a *higher* bar: any employee or family member posting about Freestyle must disclose the employment relationship, every time, and must not post retail-site reviews at all.)

## 4. Worry list, ranked

1. **Comparative absorbency claims** ("55% more than #1 brand") — the single most likely NAD challenge from P&G/Kimberly-Clark, per Coterie. Substantiation file must be airtight and current.
2. **Rash/health language drift** — creators converting "keeps skin dry" into "cured the rash." FDA drug-claim line + FTC health-claim substantiation (cf. FTC v. Gerber over infant allergy-prevention claims).
3. **"Free of allergens"/hypoallergenic exposure** — class-action bait if composition ever changes (Huggies).
4. **Undisclosed gifting** — the highest-volume risk; most creators under-disclose gifted product.
5. **Eco-claim creep** — "sustainable/biodegradable" appearing in creator captions or our own social copy without Green Guides substantiation (gDiapers).
6. **Reviews Rule** — any program that seeds retail reviews with free product needs disclosure baked in; never condition anything on positive sentiment.
